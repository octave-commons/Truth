# rendering diffuse non uniform distrobutions of gases shaders

For diffuse, non-uniform gas, the right mental model is a heterogeneous participating medium rather than a surface with a normal map or ordinary diffuse BRDF. Render it by ray marching through a 3D density field and accumulating transmittance, absorption, and in-scattered light along the view ray.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

## Core model

“Heterogeneous” just means the medium varies across space, which is exactly what gives you clumps, wisps, voids, and soft gradients instead of a uniform fog slab. In volumetric rendering, the phase function takes the role that a BRDF plays for surfaces, because the light is scattering within the volume instead of bouncing from a well-defined boundary.[^1_1]

For the non-uniform part, build density as a field, not as a shell; practical cloud renderers commonly use a large-scale tiling noise for shape and a smaller moving noise layer for turbulence and breakup. That is why volumetric gas usually feels convincing only when the shader is sampling a changing density field through space, not just tinting pixels by depth.[^1_2][^1_4]

## Diffuse look

If you want the gas to read as soft and diffuse, keep the scattering close to isotropic rather than strongly directional. In the Henyey-Greenstein model, $g=0$ is isotropic, positive $g$ biases scattering forward, and negative $g$ biases it backward.[^1_5][^1_6]

That gives you a useful artistic control: gas that should feel milky or evenly lit stays near isotropic, while gas that should glow around a light source uses more forward scattering. When one lobe is not enough, a double Henyey-Greenstein model can combine forward and backward behavior in the same medium.[^1_6][^1_5]

## Shader shape

A common real-time pattern is one ray per pixel with evenly spaced samples through the volume, accumulating opacity and color as the ray advances. This works well for clouds and gas because there is no hard surface to intersect first; the visible form emerges from the density field itself.[^1_4][^1_1]

You usually want early termination once the ray is almost fully blocked, because further samples contribute very little while still costing time. The same sampling scheme also tends to give nearby gas more apparent fidelity than distant gas, which is often acceptable for real-time work.[^1_4]

## Truth fit

For Truth, your existing design already treats atmosphere as simulation data per geodesic cell, with fields like temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, cloud cover, and precipitation, so those values are a strong base for gas density instead of relying on noise alone. That means the “shape” of the gas can come from the world simulation first, with procedural noise only adding sub-cell breakup and motion.[^1_7]

Your renderer plan already casts rays from the camera into the sphere and resolves world state from a cell lookup, so a future GPU version can extend that idea by marching through an atmosphere volume before the surface sample rather than faking gas as a flat overlay. That also matches your broader rendering direction of owning the render loop and shader pipeline directly instead of hiding it behind a higher-level engine abstraction.[^1_8][^1_7]

# it seems like the gas collapses too quickly into stars, like the nebula never really gets it's circular movement, the planets all end up these fixed things in the middle, the stars never orbit each other. it seems like we need to simulate more gas? You’re seeing the gas “fall straight in” and freeze because your current model is missing the ingredients that, in reality, sustain rotation, substructure, and long-lived gas around stars. Academic simulations of star formation almost always combine gravity with turbulence, pressure/thermodynamics, magnetic fields, and stellar feedback; those features are what produce rotating discs, orbiting stars, and nebulae that don’t just instantly collapse into one central clump.[^2_1][^2_2][^2_3]

## How star formation actually works

Stars form in cold, dense molecular clouds—10–100 pc structures with masses of $10^4$–$10^6\,M_\odot$, temperatures of $\sim 10$ K, and highly supersonic turbulence. Observations of clouds like G148.24+00.41 show that gravity dominates in dense hubs but the magnetic field and turbulence still matter, producing filaments, clumps, and converging flows instead of a single monolithic collapse. Simulations and observations agree that star formation proceeds hierarchically: the cloud breaks into many subclusters that later merge rather than collapsing as one smooth sphere.[^2_4][^2_1][^2_2][^2_3]

## Collapse, angular momentum, and disks

Real clouds are never perfectly non-rotating; even tiny net angular momentum becomes crucial as the cloud collapses. As gas contracts, conservation of angular momentum spins up the central region, creating a rotating protostellar disc instead of a static ball, and feeding the nascent star(s) through accretion. Detailed SPH calculations of molecular core collapse show bar formation, disc formation, and fragmentation into multiple objects, with rotation and anisotropy surviving well past the first collapse phase. At cluster scale, gas removal and mergers often enhance rotation and anisotropic expansion rather than erasing them, so young clusters are expected to rotate and expand rather than sit as fixed points.[^2_5][^2_6][^2_7][^2_8]

## Why nebulae don’t just “vanish into stars”

Empirically, star formation is inefficient: only a few percent of a cloud’s mass becomes stars before feedback and dynamics disperse the rest. Turbulence creates transient overdensities that may collapse locally but also drives kinetic support, preventing the whole cloud from collapsing on one free-fall time. Magnetic fields and feedback (ionizing radiation, stellar winds, supernovae) carve cavities, launch outflows, and push gas away, slowing and sometimes halting further collapse even in regions where gravity is locally dominant.[^2_9][^2_1][^2_6][^2_2][^2_10][^2_3]

## How academic simulations treat gas and stars

Modern star-formation simulations couple hydrodynamics for gas with N-body dynamics for stars rather than treating everything as collisionless particles. Many use SPH or moving-mesh codes (e.g. Phantom, AREPO, Enzo-Abyss) to evolve gas with pressure, cooling, heating, and sometimes magnetic fields, on top of self-gravity. When gas reaches high density, it is converted to “sink particles” that represent forming stars or subclusters; these sinks accrete gas, feel gravity, and can merge, capturing realistic spatial and kinematic structure from the gas rather than being dropped at the exact center by hand.[^2_9][^2_5][^2_11][^2_12][^2_3]

Feedback is usually modeled with subgrid prescriptions: H II region expansion, radiation pressure, stellar winds, and supernovae inject energy and momentum into surrounding gas, driving expansion and dispersal instead of allowing unlimited infall. Hybrid hydro/direct N-body approaches go even further by evolving the star cluster and dense gas self-consistently, allowing runaway collisions, very massive stars, and black hole seeds while still keeping the gas dynamics realistic.[^2_11][^2_6][^2_13][^2_9]

## Stellar dynamics, orbits, and multiple stars

Once stars exist, their orbits are evolved with direct N-body or softened gravity methods, often starting from hydrodynamical outputs that encode rotation, substructure, and anisotropy from the cloud phase. These simulations show that subclusters collide and merge over Myr timescales; depending on collision speed, the stellar component may remain in multiple pieces, while gas can form a monolithic central structure, so you rarely get “one static blob in the middle” as a realistic outcome. At galactic scale, codes like AREPO show continuous radial redistribution of stellar orbits driven by spiral structure and gas dynamics—stars change guiding center radii and eccentricities over time instead of sitting on fixed circular paths.[^2_5][^2_4][^2_12][^2_14]

## Why your nebula collapses too fast and looks static

Compared to the academic setups, your Phase 0 nebula and early stellar mechanics likely differ in several key ways:

- **No or minimal initial angular momentum.** If the initial gas distribution is spherically symmetric with near-zero net rotation, pure gravity will drive a radially inward collapse that piles mass at the center instead of building a disc or orbiting substructure.[^2_7][^2_15]
- **No turbulence or velocity substructure.** Real clouds start with a turbulent velocity field; without that, you lose filamentary collapse, local fragment support, and the time delays that prevent “all-at-once” infall.[^2_2][^2_10][^2_3]
- **Gas treated as pressureless particles.** If you’re modeling nebular gas as cold point masses under gravity only, you’re missing thermal pressure and radiative cooling/heating, which in real clouds create quasi-stable configurations (virialized or trans-Alfvénic) rather than free-fall everywhere.[^2_1][^2_8]
- **Instant or overly aggressive conversion to stars.** Turning dense gas into stars immediately and centrally—without a sink accretion phase or feedback—can drain the gas reservoir and freeze the star distribution into a static configuration far faster than in realistic simulations.[^2_5][^2_3]
- **No feedback carving out structure.** Without feedback, there are no H II bubbles, outflows, or shells to maintain an extended nebula; all gas just keeps falling in until your numerical scheme saturates.[^2_9][^2_6]

Those choices produce exactly the behavior you describe: gas collapsing quickly into one or a few central “fixed things,” planets/stars not orbiting each other, and nebulae that never develop coherent circular motion.

## What this implies for Truth’s stellar phase

Your Phase 0 design already names gravity, temperature, pressure, chemistry, and fusion as core domains, with nebular regions, resolved clusters, and orbiting bodies in the minimal world state. Academic work suggests those domains are sufficient in principle, but only if you treat them as active fields—turbulent velocity, thermal support, and magnetic/feedback effects—rather than passive scalars on particles that purely follow gravity. You also already plan a dual representation where unfocused matter is statistical and focused matter is voxel/particle; that maps well onto the “hybrid hydro + sink particle” approaches used in current simulations if your statistical field encodes turbulence and rotation instead of just density.[^2_9][^2_5][^2_1][^2_3][^2_8][^2_16]

For multi-star behavior, cluster simulations show that subcluster mergers, anisotropic expansion, and rotation persist even after most gas is removed, so your stellar systems should keep evolving and orbiting in the Truth timeline rather than locking into a fixed pattern once the nebular phase ends. That’s especially important for your later phases, where planetary orbits and civilizational histories depend on long-lived dynamical structure rather than “one central star plus frozen planets”.[^2_5][^2_4][^2_6][^2_12][^2_16]

## Concrete knobs to bring your model closer to the literature

Translating the academic patterns into engine-level decisions, you can push your nebula away from instant collapse and toward realistic rotation and long-lived gas by:

- **Seeding angular momentum and turbulence at initialization.** Sample the nebular velocity field from a turbulent spectrum (e.g. Kolmogorov-like) with a small but non-zero global spin, so collapse produces filaments and discs rather than radial infall.[^2_2][^2_10][^2_3]
- **Giving gas a proper equation of state.** Track temperature and pressure, allow heating from compression and cooling from expansion, and aim for clouds that are near virial equilibrium or “hierarchical collapse” rather than free-fall everywhere.[^2_8][^2_2]
- **Using sink particles for stars and clusters.** When gas exceeds a density threshold, create sinks that accrete gas over time, inherit local velocity, and form stars/subclusters with realistic spatial and kinematic distributions—don’t teleport stars into the center.[^2_9][^2_5][^2_3]
- **Adding minimal feedback.** Even a simple H II bubble model (pressure-driven expansion around massive sinks) will keep pockets of gas from collapsing, maintaining a visible nebula and opening cavities instead of allowing complete infall.[^2_6][^2_9]
- **Preserving and evolving cluster-scale rotation.** Track total angular momentum of stellar groups and let mergers and gas accretion enhance or erase rotation as in cluster simulations, so your early clusters spin and expand rather than staying static.[^2_5][^2_4][^2_6]

## Design goals

The EM field system should (1) slow and shape collapse via magnetic pressure/tension, (2) channel gas along filaments and into rotating discs, and (3) preserve extended nebulae and multi-star structures consistent with observed molecular clouds and cluster formation simulations. It must integrate cleanly with Truth’s four-quadrant architecture (domain/shape/law/infra), remain data-oriented and testable, and support Phase 0 (nebula) up through planetary and civilizational phases without being rewritten.[^3_2][^3_3][^3_4][^3_5][^3_1]

## Physical regimes to support

At minimum the EM design needs to cover three scales: (1) giant molecular cloud / nebula (tens of parsecs, magnetized turbulent gas), (2) protostellar disc and embedded cluster (AU–pc scales, rotationally supported, partially ionized gas), and (3) planetary magnetospheres and ionospheres (interaction of stellar wind with planetary fields). Academic simulations emphasize that magnetic fields are already significant in dense hubs of GMCs, where they are transcritical/supercritical but still shape filamentary inflows and clump formation, so Truth’s EM model must be active from the first frame of Phase 0 rather than switched on “later”.[^3_4][^3_6][^3_1][^3_2]

## Domain decomposition

Introduce a dedicated `domain.em` namespace that owns the large-scale magnetic field evolution, Lorentz forces on gas, and EM energy bookkeeping, never doing any IO itself. Hydrodynamics and gravity stay in `domain.stellar` / `domain.atmosphere` for nebular gas and later planetary atmospheres, with EM coupling expressed as explicit force and energy terms passed between domains each tick. Infra-side visualization of field lines, aurora, etc. belongs in `infra.render` and `infra.debug`, which consume pure field data and never compute physics themselves.[^3_3][^3_7][^3_2]

## Shape and law layer responsibilities

`shape.em` defines pure geometric operations and coordinate transforms for fields on the icosphere grid: representation of $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{E}$, curls, divergences, and projections between spherical coordinates and Cartesian vectors used by the renderer. `law.em` defines Malli schemas and contracts for field records (e.g. “each cell must have finite $\mathbf{B}$ and energy consistent with density and velocity”), EM stability checks (e.g. no unbounded amplification in a single tick), and coupling invariants with `domain.stellar` (e.g. energy exchange between kinetic, thermal, and magnetic components remains within tolerance).[^3_7][^3_3][^3_4]

## Data structures for gas and fields

In Phase 0, the nebular gas is represented by a geodesic grid of cells keyed by `cell-id`, each carrying density, velocity, temperature, chemical composition, and a magnetic field vector $\mathbf{B}$. This matches the observational picture of GMC hubs where dust polarization maps reveal coherent field orientations, decreasing polarization toward denser clumps, and trans-Alfvénic mass-to-flux ratios. In the immediate LOD zone, finer voxel/particle representations resolve detailed filaments and discs, but they still reference the coarse-grid field for large-scale constraints, avoiding a second incompatible EM representation.[^3_5][^3_1][^3_2][^3_3]

This combination ensures that collapse happens hierarchically into filaments and clumps, while fields channel material into hubs and discs instead of allowing instant radial infall.[^3_8][^3_1][^3_2][^3_4]

## EM evolution model (ideal MHD-lite)

For large-scale nebula dynamics, Truth can adopt a simplified ideal MHD model: evolve $\mathbf{B}$ via the induction equation, couple magnetic pressure and tension into the momentum equation, and optionally include a resistive term to allow slow diffusion in dense regions. Academic SPH and grid codes routinely use MHD to study collapsing cores and find that magnetic support, misalignment between rotation and field, and ambipolar diffusion strongly affect disc sizes and fragmentation, even when the field is only transcritical. In practice, Truth’s `domain.em/tick-em!` will compute:[^3_9][^3_4][^3_7]

- Updated $\mathbf{B}$ per cell from velocity and existing field
- Magnetic pressure $P_B \propto B^2$ and tension forces
- Source terms for kinetic and thermal energy (e.g. via reconnection or compression)

and then emit a “field-force” packet that `domain.stellar` consumes to update gas velocities before gravity and hydrodynamics run.[^3_2][^3_4]

## Gravity–EM–hydro tick order

To avoid the “all gas falls straight in” behavior, the tick order in Phase 0 should be:

1. **Gravity:** compute self-gravity and potential.
2. **EM:** compute field evolution and Lorentz forces given current velocities and density.
3. **Hydro:** update gas velocity and density including pressure, turbulence driving, and EM forces.
4. **Thermodynamics:** update temperature and cooling/heating.
5. **Star/sink formation:** apply thresholds to spawn sink particles (stars, subclusters) and adjust gas.

This ordering reflects academic practice where gravity, magnetic fields, and turbulence jointly regulate collapse; EM and pressure forces can partially counteract gravity on each step, producing slow, filamentary infall rather than one free-fall plunge. It also lets you enforce law-level contracts each tick ensuring no single subsystem drives the state outside physically plausible bounds.[^3_4][^3_7][^3_2]

## Star and cluster formation pipeline

Truth’s star-formation logic should follow the “sink particle in magnetized gas” pattern: dense magnetized clumps that exceed a collapse threshold create sink particles that inherit local position, velocity, and angular momentum from the EM–hydro state. These sinks accrete gas over Myr of simulated time, feel N-body gravity, and form embedded clusters whose rotation and anisotropy reflect the mergers and gas removal dynamics seen in coupled hydro/N-body simulations. EM feedback—via protostellar jets and outflows aligned with local fields—can be encoded as limited mass and momentum ejection from sinks back into surrounding gas, helping clear cavities and preserve nebular structure instead of letting everything collapse everywhere.[^3_10][^3_11][^3_12][^3_13][^3_2]

## Nebula persistence and feedback

To keep nebulae visually and physically present, the EM field must participate in feedback-driven gas removal and shaping: H II regions, bubbles, and shells take on magnetized morphologies, with compressed fields along ionization fronts and evacuated cavities behind massive stars. This is consistent with observations where U-shaped field morphologies indicate converging accretion flows and feedback-shaped structures near massive clumps. Truth can model this at coarse scale as enhanced pressure and reduced density in feedback zones, with EM providing anisotropy (easier expansion along weak-field directions) so gas isn’t removed spherically but along realistic paths.[^3_11][^3_13][^3_1]

## Multi-star and orbital behavior

With gas, gravity, and EM all active, multi-star systems emerge naturally from clump fragmentation and subcluster merger instead of being placed by hand. Cluster-scale rotation and anisotropic expansion should be preserved by the N-body module, as large-scale simulations show that merger-driven rotation survives gas removal and looks qualitatively like clusters that underwent violent relaxation. - Compute magnetic pressure/tension forces per cell.

Academic simulations show that whether ideal or non-ideal MHD dominates, magnetic pressure and torques stabilize discs, reduce fragmentation, and control angular momentum transport. Your tick contract can deliberately encode that EM’s job is “make collapse anisotropic and slow enough to form discs and filaments,” without needing full-blown non-ideal microphysics in version one.[^4_7][^4_8][^4_9]

### 2.2 Tick ordering in Phase 0 loop

Within the Phase 0 loop (nebula time), a canonical sequence:

1. `domain.orbital/tick-orbit!` — large-scale gravity, including galactic tidal terms if you add them later.[^4_5][^4_10]
2. `domain.em/tick-em!` — field evolution \& Lorentz forces.
3. `domain.stellar/tick-gas!` — hydro update, applying gravity + EM + pressure.
4. `domain.thermo/tick-thermo!` — temperature, cooling/heating.
5. `domain.stellar/tick-sinks!` — star/cluster formation and accretion, with feedback hooks.

This mirrors the way core-collapse and disc formation studies integrate gravity, EM, and thermodynamics, where magnetic braking and outflows regulate angular momentum and flux during collapse.[^4_11][^4_3]

## 3. Non-ideal effects and angular momentum

Even in present-day star formation, **non-ideal MHD** (Ohmic dissipation, ambipolar diffusion, Hall effect) is required to avoid overly strong fields and the “magnetic braking catastrophe” that suppresses disc formation. Your design should account for this at the level of contracts and eventual upgrades.[^4_8][^4_11]

### 3.1 First-pass: ideal MHD with soft caps

Initial implementation:

- Treat EM as ideal MHD for large-scale nebula dynamics.
- Use law-level caps on field amplification at protostellar densities to avoid inserting unrealistically strong fields into stellar cores, which is a known issue in pure ideal runs.[^4_8][^4_3]
- Reserve a “non-ideal hook” for later (density/ionization dependent resistivity, ambipolar diffusion term) so the design doesn’t block future realism upgrades.[^4_9][^4_11]

This acknowledges the physics that ideal MHD over-implants fields and suppresses discs, but lets you ship a playable system before fully solving the magnetic flux and braking problems.

### 3.2 Angular momentum contracts

Because the Hall effect and braking directly affect rotation, you want domain-level invariants:

- Track total angular momentum of each core / subcluster.
- Require that EM + gravity + feedback only move angular momentum within physically plausible ranges, rather than generating or destroying large amounts numerically.[^4_12][^4_13]
- Allow **magnetic braking** to move angular momentum outward, but ensure disc formation isn’t totally suppressed except in intentionally extreme configurations.[^4_12][^4_11]

This keeps multi-star formation and disc behavior in the “observed and simulated” envelope, where some rotations survive and discs exist, not in a degenerate zero-spin world.

## 4. Sinks, discs, and outflows

### 4.1 Sink particle behavior in magnetized gas

As in the cluster-formation literature, Truth should treat protostars and subclusters as sink particles that accrete magnetized gas and inherit angular momentum and local EM topology. Design rules:[^4_14][^4_15][^4_3]

- Sinks store: mass, position, velocity, net angular momentum, and a local field sample.
- Accretion of gas modifies those properties and can launch outflows if EM + thermal criteria are met.[^4_16][^4_3][^4_11]
- Sinks should not instantly erase surrounding gas; instead, they carve magnetized pseudo-discs and jets, leaving nebular gas present and dynamic.[^4_3][^4_11]


### 4.2 Protostellar jets and feedback

Radiation MHD simulations of core collapse show outflows launched from both first cores and stellar cores, driven by magnetic and thermal forces. You can abstract this in Truth as:[^4_16][^4_3]

- When a sink’s accretion rate and local field exceed thresholds, spawn an outflow event along the local field direction.
- Outflows remove gas along these paths, shaping cavities and filaments while preserving extended nebula elsewhere.[^4_3][^4_6]
- Outflow events write to your Merkle-DAG ledger as EM+mass-momentum transactions, preserving replayability and causality.

This connects Phase 0 events (jets carving nebula) with later planetary and civilizational experiences (heritage cavities, star-forming regions, and field topology).[^4_2][^4_17]

## 5. Multi-physics disc formation

### 5.1 Magnetorotational instability and disc structure

For later phases, discs around stars are governed by gravity, rotation, and EM-driven instabilities like MRI, which affect accretion bursts and planet formation prospects. Design-wise:[^4_18][^4_19]

- Treat the disc as a 2D/3D grid with density, velocity, and $\mathbf{B}$ inherited from the cloud collapse.
- Use simplified MRI triggers (based on temperature/ionization thresholds) to episodically enhance angular momentum transport and accretion, creating bursty evolution consistent with simulations.[^4_19][^4_18]
- Keep these events tied back to the same EM fields that existed in Phase 0, so the story from nebula to disc is continuous.


### 5.2 Planet formation difficulty and feedback

Because MRI bursts and GI can disrupt dust rings and make streaming instability harder, your disc design can let some worlds struggle to form planets, aligning with recent thin-disk MHD results. That fits Truth’s design tension: some cosmologies will naturally lead to rich planetary systems; others will be geologically active but sparsely planetary, and the player’s early EM choices and attention patterns will influence which branch they witness.[^4_18][^4_2]

# How about the star formation event? the protostar, the ignition of fusion, the possible outcome (star classes) solar winds, the voxels of the planets colliding.

Treat the “star formation event” as a staged transition in your magnetized sink + voxel world: a collapsing core becomes a protostar, crosses deuterium-burning, then hydrogen ignition, and is promoted to a main-sequence star whose class, winds, and later planetary collisions all emerge from the same underlying mass/EM/chemistry state.[^5_1][^5_2][^5_3]

## Protostar: from collapsing core to first burning

Observationally and in simulations, a protostar is the opaque central object that forms in a collapsing core before central hydrogen burning, while accretion and deuterium burning dominate its luminosity.[^5_4][^5_5]

- Collapse produces a “first core”: a few AU in size, $\sim 0.05\,M_\odot$, where gas becomes optically thick and the isothermal approximation breaks down; temperature rises steadily as matter piles on.[^5_4]
- Deuterium burning ignites around $10^6$ K, acting as a thermostat: it slows contraction, can swell the radius, and its behavior depends on whether the core is fully convective or begins to develop radiative zones.[^5_1][^5_6]

In Truth, that maps nicely to:

- A **SinkCore** record that becomes a `Protostar` once central density and temperature cross a “first core” threshold.
- A deuterium-burning phase where accretion luminosity plus nuclear luminosity are tracked separately, matching the idea that protostar luminosity is dominated by accretion shocks until hydrogen ignition.[^5_1][^5_4]

You already use voxel/particle representations for gas in focus; the protostar is just a sink with a small voxel envelope where accretion shocks generate light and where EM fields begin to concentrate.[^5_3]

## Ignition of fusion and promotion to star

Hydrogen burning requires core temperatures $\sim 10^7$ K, with the pp-chain dominating at lower masses and the CNO cycle at higher masses; once hydrogen burning balances radiation losses, contraction halts and the object arrives on the “birthline” and main sequence.[^5_1][^5_5][^5_2]

Design-wise:

- Track central temperature and composition in the SinkCore; when hydrogen burning rate is sufficient to balance radiative loss, flip a `fusion-state` flag from `none` → `D-burning` → `H-burning`.
- On `H-burning`, promote `Protostar` to `Star`, attach a long-lived core burning phase and mark the time as the “birthline” in your world journal, consistent with pre-main-sequence models.[^5_5][^5_6]

This event is where you also lock in the **initial mass** that will determine spectral type and lifetime, because in practice stellar mass at hydrogen ignition largely fixes structure for the rest of the main sequence.[^5_2]

## Star classes and their simulation meaning

Main-sequence spectral types (O, B, A, F, G, K, M) are essentially a mass and effective temperature classification: massive stars are hot, luminous, short-lived; low-mass stars are cool and long-lived.[^5_7][^5_2]

You can encode:

- A mass-based mapping at birthline: e.g.
    - $M \lesssim 0.5\,M_\odot$ → M/K dwarfs, convective, pp-chain only.
    - $\sim 1\,M_\odot$ → G-type (Sun-like).
    - $>1.5\,M_\odot$ → A/B types, radiative envelopes, CNO-dominated cores.
    - $>8\,M_\odot$ → O/B types with short main-sequence lifetimes, strong winds, later core-collapse supernova.[^5_2]
- Attach to each class: luminosity, spectrum (colour/energy distribution), wind properties, expected lifetime and end state (white dwarf, neutron star, black hole).[^5_2]

That gives Truth a way to let early collapse outcomes (IMF shape, mass distribution of sinks) determine the later stellar population and thus the entire ecological and civilizational affordance space.[^5_3]

## Solar wind and planetary atmospheres

Once a star is on the main sequence, it launches a steady solar/stellar wind: a stream of charged particles plus XUV/EUV radiation that ionizes, heats, chemically modifies, and slowly erodes the upper atmospheres of nearby planets.[^5_8][^5_9]

- The forcing scales roughly with $1/r^2$ in distance, so inner worlds are strongly affected; gravity and intrinsic dipole magnetic fields act as shields, while weakly magnetized planets (Mars, Venus, Titan) suffer ion pickup, sputtering, and plasma erosion.[^5_10][^5_8]
- Earth’s strong dipole pushes the solar wind out to ~10 Earth radii, forming a “magnetic umbrella,” whereas Mars and Venus show strong transport of energized ionospheric plasma to the nightside because their induced fields are too weak to prevent waves and plasma access.[^5_8][^5_10]

In your engine:

- Each `Star` generates a radial wind field plus radiation field.
- Each `Planet` carries gravity, atmosphere, and intrinsic/induced EM; `domain.em`/`domain.atmosphere` then simulate ionization, heating, and escape consistent with simple comparative models (strong dipole → protected, weak/none → atmospheric loss).[^5_9][^5_3]

That directly connects your EM design to planetary habitability and long-term atmospheric evolution, instead of treating winds as decorative.

## Voxels, planetesimals, and collisions

Planets (and smaller bodies) form from the protoplanetary disc: solids grow from dust to km-scale planetesimals, then collide and merge via gravity and disc dynamics. In your architecture, these are naturally voxel aggregates:

- Disc solids are represented as bonded voxel masses with local composition, temperature, and velocity.
- Collisions between bodies are resolved as voxel–voxel interactions: depending on impact speed and angle, they accrete, fragment, or partially melt, similar to numerical experiments on core formation and giant impacts in planetary science (you can keep the actual physics reduced but the event types recognizable).

Because your substrate is “voxels everywhere,” the same collision engine can handle:

- Planetesimal growth and destructive collisions.
- Giant impacts that reshape planets and spawn moons.
- Smaller meteoroid impacts that alter surface geology and atmospheres.

Coupling to EM and solar wind:

- Charged dust and plasma near the star respond to $\mathbf{B}$ and wind, affecting migration and collision statistics.
- Post-impact atmospheres subject to strong winds will lose volatiles faster if the planet lacks a protective field, tying collision history and EM state into ecology and later civilizations.[^5_8][^5_10][^5_3]


## Putting it together as a single event pipeline

In Truth terms, the “star formation event” isn’t a single flip but a pipeline:

1. **Magnetized core collapse:** sink + EM + hydro evolve from cloud to first core, with deuterium burning acting as a thermostat and feedback carving jets and cavities.[^5_1][^5_3][^5_11]
2. **Protostar phase:** accretion dominates luminosity, EM funnels gas through pseudo-discs, deuterium burning alters radius and entropy depending on mass.[^5_6][^5_1]
3. **Hydrogen ignition:** core reaches $\sim 10^7$ K; fusion balances radiation; object is promoted to `Star` with class based on mass, structure, and composition.[^5_5][^5_2][^5_1]
4. **Wind and radiation:** star launches solar wind and XUV/EUV; EM field interacts with planetary magnetospheres and atmospheres, controlling escape, chemistry, and plasma dynamics.[^5_8][^5_9][^5_10]
5. **Disc and collisions:** protoplanetary voxels collide and merge under gravity, EM and wind shape dust and gas; planets and moons emerge as voxel aggregates with histories encoded in your ledger.[^5_3]

# We need the math, the equations of stellar then planetary formation. temperature, magnetic, gravitational, light fields. The full physics must be present, and determining which model of physics is most dominate at a given location in space. the end of phase 0 means we have a planet with a molten core, volcanisim, techntonics, magnetic fields, electric fields.

how those play with the gases of the atmosphere

You can think of Phase 0 as “turning on” a coupled gravity–hydro–MHD–thermal system and then watching which regime dominates at each point in space. Below is a compact set of equations you can actually implement, plus the dimensionless diagnostics that tell you whether gravity, pressure, magnetic fields, or radiation are in charge locally, and how that carries through into planet interior convection, tectonics, and atmosphere–field coupling.[^6_1][^6_2][^6_3][^6_4][^6_5][^6_6][^6_7][^6_8]

***

## Core field variables

Per cell (or voxel) you need at least:

- Mass density $\rho$
- Velocity $\mathbf{v}$
- Gas pressure $p$
- Internal energy per mass $e$
- Magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$
- Gravitational potential $\Phi$
- Radiation field / light energy density $E_{\rm rad}$ (coarse)
- Composition, temperature $T$, ionization fraction $x_e$

These live both in nebular gas and later in atmospheres and planetary interiors; the physics changes mainly via the parameters and dominant timescales, not the form of the equations.

***

## Stellar formation: gravity + MHD + thermodynamics

### Gravity and collapse

Self-gravity is set by Poisson’s equation:

$$
\nabla^2 \Phi = 4 \pi G \rho
$$

Gas feels gravity via the force $-\rho \nabla \Phi$ in the momentum equation.[^6_3][^6_9]

Collapse vs support is determined by Jeans criteria; the **Jeans length**:

$$
\lambda_J = \sqrt{\frac{\pi c_s^2}{G \rho}}, \quad c_s = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma p}{\rho}}
$$

Regions with size $L > \lambda_J$ are gravitationally unstable and tend to collapse; smaller regions are pressure supported.[^6_10]

### Ideal MHD equations

For the magnetized nebula, the simplest full physics model is **ideal MHD** (plus cooling):

- **Continuity (mass conservation)**

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\rho \mathbf{v}) = 0
$$
- **Momentum (Newton’s law for fluid)**

$$
\rho\left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{v}\right)
 = -\nabla p - \rho \nabla \Phi + \frac{1}{4\pi}(\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) \times \mathbf{B}
$$

The last term is the Lorentz force, which can be decomposed into magnetic pressure and tension.[^6_1][^6_3]
- **Induction (magnetic field evolution)**
Ideal Ohm’s law $\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}/c = 0$ combined with Faraday’s law gives:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B})
$$

plus the constraint $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$.[^6_5][^6_3][^6_1]
- **Energy (internal + kinetic + magnetic)**
In one convenient form:

$$
\frac{\partial e}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla e
 = -\frac{p}{\rho} \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} + \text{heating} - \text{cooling}
$$

where “heating” includes compressional heating, shocks, and nuclear burning once conditions are met; “cooling” is radiative and (later) neutrino cooling in very dense cores.[^6_11][^6_12]

You can extend the induction equation with a resistive term $\eta \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}$ for non-ideal MHD (ambipolar diffusion, Ohmic dissipation) once you want better flux removal in dense cores.[^6_13][^6_5]

### Fusion ignition conditions

In your sink/Protostar object, you track central density and temperature via the same energy equation. Hydrogen burning starts once:

- Central $T_c \sim 10^7 \,\mathrm{K}$
- Sufficient density and composition for pp-chain or CNO cycles

At that point, nuclear power $\epsilon_{\rm nuc}(\rho, T)$ enters the heating term and is allowed to balance radiative losses, halting contraction and placing the object on the main sequence.[^6_14][^6_15][^6_16]

***

## Planet formation in discs: Toomre Q and cooling

Once you have a protostellar disc, self-gravity vs pressure vs rotation is captured by the **Toomre Q parameter**:[^6_2]

$$
Q(R) = \frac{c_s(R)\,\kappa(R)}{\pi G \Sigma(R)}
$$

- $\Sigma(R)$ is disc surface density.
- $c_s(R)$ is sound speed (set by disc temperature).
- $\kappa(R)$ is epicyclic frequency (≈ orbital frequency $\Omega$ in a Keplerian disc).

Key regime classifier:

- $Q \gtrsim 1$: disc stable against axisymmetric self-gravity at radius $R$.
- $Q \lesssim 1$: disc locally unstable; can form spiral arms and fragment into clumps/planets.

Fragmentation also requires sufficiently fast cooling: Gammie’s criterion $t_{\rm cool} \lesssim 3 \Omega^{-1}$ in thin discs, or the exact factor from your chosen cooling model. So per disc cell you can evaluate $Q$ and $t_{\rm cool}$ and decide whether the dominant regime is “smooth disc + core accretion” or “GI + clump formation”.[^6_2]

Terrestrial planet growth, collisions, and migration can then be modeled with N-body gravity for planetesimals plus disc torques. You don’t need extra field equations—just gravitational interaction and drag/torque sourced by the disc’s hydrodynamics.[^6_2]

***

## Planet interior: convection, mantle, core

By the end of Phase 0 you want a planet with:

- Molten or partially molten core
- Convecting mantle
- Surface volcanism and tectonics
- Global magnetic field and electric currents


### Mantle convection: Rayleigh number

Mantle convection is governed by the same physics as any heated fluid layer: buoyancy vs viscosity vs thermal diffusion. The key dimensionless number is the **Rayleigh number**:[^6_4][^6_6]

$$
Ra = \frac{\rho g \alpha \Delta T d^3}{\mu \kappa}
$$

- $\rho$ — density
- $g$ — gravity at mantle depth
- $\alpha$ — thermal expansivity
- $\Delta T$ — temperature contrast across the convecting layer
- $d$ — layer thickness (mantle depth)
- $\mu$ — dynamic viscosity
- $\kappa$ — thermal diffusivity

Regimes:

- $Ra < Ra_c$ (critical, ≈ O(10^3) for simple geometries): conduction dominates, no convection; planet is tectonically dead.
- $Ra > Ra_c$: convection occurs, with vigor increasing with $Ra$; large $Ra$ leads to turbulent, vigorous convection that can drive plate tectonics.[^6_17][^6_18][^6_4]

The underlying **energy equation** in the mantle is:

$$
\rho c_p \left( \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla T \right)
 = k \nabla^2 T + H_{\rm rad} - H_{\rm loss}
$$

- $c_p$ — heat capacity; $k$ — thermal conductivity.
- $H_{\rm rad}$ — volumetric heating (radioactive decay, latent heat).
- $H_{\rm loss}$ — heat loss to surface and core.

Solving this in combination with Stokes flow ($\nabla \cdot \sigma + \rho \mathbf{g} = 0$) gives mantle convection patterns that drive volcanism and tectonics.[^6_18][^6_17]

### Core dynamo: induction + rotation

The planetary magnetic field is generated by a convecting, electrically conducting fluid core. At core scales you’re again in MHD; the same induction equation applies:[^6_19][^6_11]

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \nabla \times (\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) + \eta \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}
$$

with:

- $\mathbf{v}$ — core flow velocity, influenced by rotation and buoyancy.
- $\eta = 1/(\mu_0 \sigma)$ — magnetic diffusivity, $\sigma$ electrical conductivity.

Regimes:

- If convective power (from core cooling, compositional convection) and rotation are large enough, the dynamo sustains a global dipole; that’s your “strong magnetic umbrella” case.
- If convection is weak or core is solidified, $\mathbf{B}$ decays, leaving only crustal/remanent fields; the planet behaves like Mars/Venus in its interaction with the stellar wind.[^6_7][^6_8]

You don’t need to simulate full dynamo turbulence—just ensure that core convection and rotation feed a field consistent with these regimes.

***

## Atmosphere: gas + gravity + EM + light

Now combine mantle/core with atmosphere and stellar fields.

### Hydrostatic structure and circulation

Vertical structure is set by hydrostatic equilibrium:

$$
\frac{dp}{dz} = -\rho g
$$

with an equation of state $p = \rho k_B T / \mu$ and radiative-convective energy balance. Large-scale circulation uses the Navier–Stokes equations on a rotating sphere:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{v}
 = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p - 2 \mathbf{\Omega} \times \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{g} + \text{viscosity} + \text{EM forces}
$$

where $\mathbf{\Omega}$ is planetary rotation, and “EM forces” include Lorentz terms on ionized components.[^6_20][^6_7]

### Solar wind and plasma interaction

The star’s wind interacts with planetary fields and atmospheres via MHD and kinetic processes. At a coarse level:[^6_8][^6_21][^6_7]

- Momentum equation gets an extra term where charged atmospheric particles feel $\mathbf{J} \times \mathbf{B}$ and electric fields.
- Energy equation gets heating terms (Joule heating, particle precipitation) in the upper atmosphere.

Weakly magnetized planets show strong ion escape and plasma transport:

- Solar wind dynamic pressure compresses the dayside ionosphere.
- Magnetic field draping and reconnection near the terminator lead to plasma escape on the nightside.
- Empirical or reduced models give escape rates as functions of EUV flux, wind pressure, and EM geometry.[^6_7][^6_8]

In Truth, you can evaluate:

- Whether the planet is in a **strongly magnetized regime** (core dynamo active, large dipole).
- Whether the atmosphere is **highly ionized and coupled** or mostly neutral.

and then let EM terms dominate upper-atmosphere behavior only where ionization and field strength are high.

***

## Local dominance: regime classifier

All of these equations can run everywhere, but you don’t want to simulate full MHD + detailed mantle convection where it doesn’t matter. Instead, compute simple dimensionless numbers per cell and choose dominant physics:

- **Plasma beta** (gas vs magnetic pressure):

$$
\beta = \frac{P_{\rm gas}}{P_B}
 = \frac{p}{B^2 / 8\pi}
$$
    - $\beta \gg 1$: gas pressure dominates; gravity + hydro.
    - $\beta \ll 1$: magnetic field dominates; EM shaping flows.[^6_19][^6_1]
- **Mach number** (supersonic vs subsonic):

$$
\mathcal{M} = \frac{|\mathbf{v}|}{c_s}
$$
    - $\mathcal{M} \gg 1$: shocks, compressible turbulence.
    - $\mathcal{M} \ll 1$: nearly incompressible flow.
- **Alfvén Mach number** (flow vs Alfvén speed):

$$
M_A = \frac{|\mathbf{v}|}{v_A}, \quad v_A = \frac{B}{\sqrt{4\pi \rho}}
$$
    - $M_A \ll 1$: magnetic tension and pressure strongly constrain motion.
    - $M_A \gg 1$: magnetic field is passively advected.[^6_1][^6_19]
- **Gravitational instability** (Jeans or Toomre Q):
    - Jeans $L/\lambda_J$ and disc $Q$.
- **Cooling vs dynamical time**:

$$
\frac{t_{\rm cool}}{t_{\rm dyn}} \quad \text{with} \quad t_{\rm dyn} \sim \Omega^{-1} \text{ or free-fall time}
$$

Fragmentation needs fast cooling.[^6_2]
- **Rayleigh number** (mantle convection):

$$
Ra = \frac{\rho g \alpha \Delta T d^3}{\mu \kappa}
$$
    - $Ra < Ra_c$: conduction; tectonically quiet.
    - $Ra \gg Ra_c$: vigorous convection; tectonics, volcanism.[^6_6][^6_17][^6_4]

At each location and scale (nebula cell, disc cell, mantle cell, atmosphere voxel), compute these numbers and have your engine choose:

- “Gravity–hydro” regime (Jeans unstable, $\beta \gg 1$, $M_A \gg 1$).
- “MHD-dominated” regime ($\beta \ll 1$, $M_A \lesssim 1$).
- “Radiation-dominated” regime (high $E_{\rm rad}$, strong cooling).
- “Convection/tectonics” regime (mantle $Ra \gg Ra_c$).

The equations are always present; the regime logic tells you which terms to prioritize for simulation detail and narrative.
