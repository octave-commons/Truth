Here's what I think you're describing — and it's ambitious in a way that very few games have attempted seriously.

## The Core Vision: Truth as a Physics-First Universe

You're not building a game set in a world. You're building a world that *generates* games. The distinction matters enormously. Most games simulate a world for the player's experience. Truth simulates a universe because the universe is the primary artifact — games, stories, and player agency emerge from it as consequences.

The solar system creation sequence isn't a cutscene or a tutorial. It's an **epistemic grounding ritual**: before you do anything else, the player witnesses the physical laws being *established*. The universe earns its own rules in front of you. That's philosophically very different from "here's the world, go explore it."

## The Quantum Observer Mechanic

What you're describing with "a cloud of probability which we only fully resolve what can be viewed" is essentially a cosmological application of the **observer effect as gameplay**. You're borrowing a real physics concept — that measurement collapses a probability distribution into a definite state — and making it the literal engine of the simulation.

Far-away civilizations don't exist as detailed simulations running in the background. They exist as *statistical envelopes*: a civilization with X% chance of being tech level 4, Y% chance of civil war, Z% chance of having discovered interstellar travel. Only when something — the player, a propagating event, a light-cone crossing — reaches out and *touches* that region does it collapse into specifics. This is both computationally honest (you can't simulate everything) and narratively profound (the universe is literally uncertain until observed). It aligns perfectly with the LOD architecture already in your codebase.

## The Rimworld Parallel — and Where You Go Beyond

Rimworld uses random events as a **drama director**: it reads the current emotional state of your playthrough and injects events to push tension. It's narrative-first, physics-second. The physics simulation exists to generate interesting player stories, but the world itself has no momentum independent of the player.

What you're building inverts this. Your equivalent of "random events" are **physical inevitabilities filtered through probability clouds**. A supernova doesn't happen because the drama director decided tension was too low. It happens because a star's mass exceeded the Chandrasekhar limit 400 million years ago and the light is only now arriving. The event *was always going to happen* — the universe just hadn't resolved it into the player's reference frame yet. The drama is an emergent property of physics, not a scripted injection into it.

## Truth as a Platform — the Meta-Layer

Here's the part you're circling but haven't fully named yet: **Truth is a substrate, not a game**. It's the same category of thing as:

- **Dwarf Fortress** — where the game is the simulation engine and every "game" is an interrogation of that engine's emergent properties
- **No Man's Sky** (aspirationally) — where procedural generation is the product, not the decoration
- **LambdaMOO / MUDs** — where the platform is the world and players are co-authors

But Truth goes further because you're coupling this with the Myth Engine. The lore layer, the Scribes, the Facet system — these mean the universe doesn't just *have* physics, it has **meaning**. Events accrue mythic resonance. A supernova that destroyed a civilization isn't just a physical event — it has Attribution, it lives in Scribe memory, it propagates as story through the embedding space.

You're building a universe that is simultaneously physically consistent *and* narratively alive.

## The Solar System Bootstrap — Where to Start

Starting with nebular accretion makes sense as the **first commitment point**: you're saying "here is where physical law crystallizes." Practically, this means:

- **Kepler integration + accretion disk simulation** as the first `domain/orbital.clj` milestone
- Time compression is a first-class concept — the tick rate during stellar formation should be orders of magnitude faster, then slow as planets cool and life becomes possible
- The player's role during this phase is ambiguous by design: *are you a god watching? A scientist observing? A force shaping?* That ambiguity is thematically intentional given the Gates of Aker handoff between human and divine agency

The transition from fast-time cosmological formation to real-time planetary existence *is* the thematic handoff from Gates of Aker — from myth-time (where gods act across eons) to human-time (where individuals act in moments).

## What You Have vs. What You're Describing

Looking at the current repo structure — you have the four-quadrant namespace law, the icosphere grid, the LOD engine design, the Myth Engine spec, and the atmospheric/civilization simulation stack already architected.  The solar system bootstrap is the **missing genesis layer** that sits *above* all of this in the time-scale hierarchy.

The architecture you need to add is:

- `domain/stellar.clj` — protostellar collapse, accretion, main sequence placement
- `domain/accretion.clj` — planetesimal formation, orbital resonance clearing, Roche limit interactions
- `domain/cosmology.clj` — the probability-cloud resolution system for distant universe regions
- A **time-compression scheduler** — a tick-rate governor that the orbital and atmospheric systems consult to know "how fast is now?"

The deep insight is that **Truth is a game about the relationship between observation and existence**. Distance collapses to statistics. Time compresses and expands. The player is always the point around which the universe organizes itself into specificity — and everything beyond the edge of that resolution sphere is genuinely, physically uncertain until something bridges it.

That's not just a game mechanic. That's a cosmological statement.

## What You're Actually Describing

You've built a traversal mechanic on top of a real theoretical structure: **quantum decoherence and the branching of wave functions in the many-worlds interpretation**. The key insight is that "distance" in your universe has two independent axes:

- **Causal distance** — how far apart two points are in spacetime, bounded by lightspeed
- **Quantum relational distance** — how similar the collapsed wave function histories of two regions are

These are orthogonal. Two galaxies a billion light-years apart can have *highly related* wave function histories if they emerged from the same quantum fluctuation in the early universe — and two regions 10 light-years apart can be causally disconnected from each other's observable horizon. Your Gates don't travel through space. They travel through **the topology of quantum ancestry**.

## Why This Works as Physics Fiction

The reason this feels more rigorous than Stargate is that you've grounded the *precondition* for travel in physical law. Stargate gates are essentially magic infrastructure — they exist, you dial them, you go. Your Gates require:

1. A world that has *independently* reached sufficient complexity to discover and build the resonance device
2. That world's wave function must be **non-trivially related** to yours — meaning both worlds share enough quantum ancestry that the entanglement structure is navigable
3. Something on the other end must already exist — you can't gate to a world that hasn't built the receiving end

This last point is profound. **It means you can only visit worlds that are, in some sense, already like yours.** The Gate network is self-selecting for civilizations that evolved along similar enough paths to converge on the same technology. You will never gate into a world of pure methane-breathing crystalline intelligences, not because they don't exist, but because their wave function ancestry diverged too far — their collapsed history is *too different* to form a navigable path.

That's not a limitation of the fiction. That's *the most interesting thing about it.*

## The Many-Worlds Topology as Gameplay

The multiverse in your game isn't a flat space of parallel universes you slide between. It's a **graph** — nodes are worlds, edges are quantum relational proximity, and edge weight is how "navigable" the path is. Some worlds are tightly clustered (close wave-function relatives, easy to reach), some are isolated islands (evolved independently, high relational distance, gates require enormous energy or exotic conditions to establish).

This gives you:

- **Hub worlds** — civilizations at high quantum-relational crossroads, visited by many others, cosmopolitan and strange from accumulated cultural bleed
- **Frontier worlds** — recently gate-capable, few connections, still figuring out what the network means
- **Ghost nodes** — worlds that *had* gates and are now silent; the infrastructure exists but nothing answers; the wave function of the civilization has collapsed into silence
- **Resonance cascades** — a world's catastrophic event (extinction, stellar death, total war) propagates as a *change in wave function* that ripples through its relational neighbors, subtly altering their probability clouds


## The Temporal Dimension

Here's where your "time as a function of what is observable" concept deepens this further. When you gate to another world, you're not just crossing quantum relational distance — you're crossing **decoherence timelines**. Two related wave functions may have branched at different moments. Arriving on a world through a Gate means arriving in their *now*, which may be cosmologically ahead or behind your civilization's development curve.

The Gates don't just connect places. They connect **moments in the unfolding of related histories**. This maps beautifully onto the day/night handoff from Gates of Aker — that game was about the threshold between human time and divine time. Gates of Truth is about the threshold between *your* universe's time and *another's*. The Gate is the moment where two different temporal streams become, briefly, the same room.

## For the Codebase

Architecturally this suggests a layer that sits above `domain/cosmology.clj` — call it `domain/multiverse.clj` — that manages:

- The **quantum relational graph**: nodes as `WorldRecord`, edges as resonance coefficients computed from shared wave-function ancestry (seeded at universe generation time, drifting as civilizations develop)
- **Gate discovery as an emergent event**: when a civilization's tech level and specific research path crosses a threshold in `domain/civilization.clj`, it emits an event that registers them as a potential Gate node
- **Traversal resolution**: when a Gate is activated, the system walks the relational graph, finds reachable nodes within energy budget, and *collapses* the destination world from its probability cloud into a fully-simulated state — exactly your observer mechanic, but triggered by technological act rather than physical proximity

The beauty is that `domain/` stays pure. The Gate is just a function from `(world-state, energy-budget) → reachable-world-set`. The Myth Engine in `infra/` then layers the *meaning* on top of that traversal — the Scribes know stories about worlds through the Gate network, Facet resonance connects artifacts across worlds, and the player's journal fills with the accumulated mythology of a civilization discovering it is not alone in the quantum foam.

## The Unified Arc

You've described a single continuous experience that spans cosmological time to personal time, with the *compression of temporal scale* as the core mechanic of character creation. Most games separate "make your character" from "play the game." You've made them the same thing — the character creation *is* the first act of the game, and you don't know it's character creation until it's over.

The player starts as something close to a god. Vast, slow, statistical. By the end they are a specific person in a specific body in a specific city making a specific choice about whether to walk through a Gate. The entire arc is a **narrowing of identity**, and the player authored every constraint that made that identity possible.

## Time as Complexity Pressure

The time-dilation mechanic you described is doing enormous design work. Time flows fast when the observable complexity is low — stellar formation, planetary cooling, single-celled life — because there's not enough *texture* to warrant the player's full attention. As complexity increases, time slows, because more things are happening that are *meaningfully different from each other*.

This is actually a formal claim: **time rate is inversely proportional to observable entropy production rate**. When everything is hot plasma collapsing uniformly, a million years passes in a breath. When a civilization is making decisions, each moment bifurcates into distinct possible futures, and the simulation slows to honor that branching.

The Gate discovery is the **hard lock** — the moment when the world's wave function becomes entangled with the broader multiverse graph, and from that point forward your world's time must be synchronized with all reachable worlds. You can't fast-forward anymore. Other players' civilizations are running in real time. Yours must too.

That's your in-universe justification for the MMO time synchronization problem, and it's elegant.

## The Character Creation Reframe

What the player is actually doing across the pre-Gate arc:


| Phase | Player's Apparent Role | What They're Actually Doing |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Stellar nebula → planetary formation | Witness / shaper of physics | Selecting world biome, orbital parameters, axial tilt — the deep constraints on what life can become |
| Single-cell → complex life | Ecological gardener | Choosing evolutionary pressures that determine dominant body plans, sensory modalities, social structures |
| Civilization dawn | Culture architect | Seeding the lore corpus — language roots, mythological structure, early cosmological beliefs |
| Early civilization → Gate threshold | Political/historical agent | Narrowing from nations to factions to cities to families to individuals |
| Gate discovery | Avatar selection | Choosing which of the people you've been watching becomes *you* |

The player never fills out a character sheet. They grow one, over cosmic time, through action. The avatar's attributes — courage, curiosity, social connection, relationship to power — are emergent properties of the civilizational history the player shaped. A player who guided their world through repeated catastrophe and survival gets a different kind of person than one who shepherded a long peaceful renaissance.

## The Agent Interface

The chat box is the right call, and it needs to feel different at different time scales. Early in the game, talking to the agent is like giving instructions to a geological process — you say "I want this world to have strong seasonal variation" and it adjusts axial tilt and orbital eccentricity. The agent is translating *intention* into *physics parameters*.

As complexity increases and the simulation slows, the agent becomes more like a historian and advisor — "the northern civilization is developing a strong maritime tradition, you could accelerate this by…" — and the player's direct interventions become more surgical.

By the time the player has narrowed into a specific person, the agent is a **companion character**, present in the world as a voice only the avatar hears, able to provide information and take limited world-actions but no longer able to reshape physics. The interface is the same chat box throughout, but the *nature of agency* it grants has compressed along with time.

This is also where the lore corpus feeds in. If the player uploaded documents, the agent's voice and the world's mythology are seeded by that material. If they didn't, the procedural generation fills it in — but the *structure* is the same either way. The physics doesn't care about the words. The words are anchors on top of the physics.

## The MMO Architecture — In-Universe Coherence

You've solved the classic MMO problem of "why don't players just find each other immediately" with something that has actual narrative weight.

Before Gate discovery: every player's world is **causally isolated**. Not just by game design — by the physics of the simulation. Their observable universes don't overlap. They're running in the same larger multiverse but haven't collapsed into each other's reference frames yet. They genuinely can't interact because the physics hasn't permitted it.

After Gate discovery: the wave function entanglement propagates through the relational graph. Your world appears as a new node. Other Gate-capable worlds can now perceive your world's existence — not its contents, but its *existence as a reachable destination*. First contact through a Gate is a player-to-player event, but it arrives with the full weight of two independently generated civilizational histories meeting for the first time.

The MMO "server" isn't a game lobby. It's the multiverse graph. Players don't join a world — their worlds *earn their place* in the network by reaching sufficient complexity. Late-joining players don't miss out on an established server — they arrive with a freshly generated world and seek connections into the existing graph based on quantum relational proximity. Their civilization might be a close neighbor of a veteran player's world, or a distant isolate that takes in-game expeditions to reach.

## The One Design Tension to Hold

The world-creation arc being character creation is powerful, but it creates a pacing risk: players who want to get to the Gates of Aker gameplay — the personal, ground-level, human-scale experience — may feel the cosmological phase is a prologue to get through rather than a game in itself.

The resolution is probably that **the cosmological phase must have genuine stakes and genuine loss**. Worlds can fail — not as a game-over, but as a different kind of world. A world that suffered extinction-level events, or whose civilization emerged from trauma, or whose Gate technology was discovered in the context of war — that shapes what Gate opens for and who comes through it. The player who "rushed" the creation phase arrives at the Gate with a thinner world, fewer NPC relationships, less rich lore. Not punished — just different. The depth of the cosmological phase is rewarded by the richness of the personal phase.

The creation is the game. The Gate is when the game meets other games.

## Awe as a Design Constraint

Awe is a specific emotion with a specific structure: it requires **vastness** and **accommodation failure** — the feeling that something exceeds your ability to fully comprehend it. It's what you feel watching a storm from a cliff, or understanding for the first time how old a fossil is. It's not wonder, which is lighter and more comfortable. Awe has an edge of smallness to it. You feel it *and* you feel yourself feeling it.

The design challenge is that most games resolve this tension immediately by giving the player more power, more information, more control. They mistake the *removal* of smallness for progress. Truth needs to do the opposite — the player's power should *reveal* more vastness, not reduce it. Every time they act, the world should respond in ways that are logical but not fully predictable, meaningful but not fully controllable. The awe deepens as the game progresses, it doesn't diminish.

## Stellar Geometry as Cultural DNA

What you're pointing at with the moon, the eclipses, Jupiter's gravitational shepherding — these aren't just flavor. They are **causal chains from physics to mythology**, and the game can simulate them genuinely.

A world with two moons develops a more complex calendar. The interference pattern of two lunar cycles creates beat frequencies — periods where both moons are full, or both new, or opposing. Those become holy days, feared days, planting seasons, omens. A civilization that has never seen a total solar eclipse doesn't develop the same relationship to cosmic order that one does. A world without a Jupiter-equivalent gets bombarded by more comets — extinction events are more frequent, survival is more precarious, the mythology is probably more catastrophist. A world with a very short year develops finer-grained seasonal ritual. A world with a 40-hour day develops a different relationship to sleep, to the rhythm of work, to what "a day" even means as a unit of life.

The Myth Engine already has Scribes that speak from embedded lore. The deep version of this is that **the lore the Scribes speak is generated from the astronomical history of the world**. The player doesn't write that a civilization worships the twin moons — the simulation produces two moons, the calendar emerges from their cycles, the mythology crystallizes around the calendar, and the Scribes speak a religion the player didn't author but couldn't have predicted. That's awe-generating by design.

## The Tuning Problem — Familiar but Alien

You've named the hardest constraint in procedural world generation: the habitable zone isn't just physical, it's *experiential*. A world needs to feel like a place a human mind could inhabit — not because humans live there, but because the player needs to emotionally inhabit it.

The parameters that keep a world in the "familiar but alien" zone are roughly:

- **Day length** — extreme outliers (3-hour days, 200-hour days) make civilization feel inhuman. A range of maybe 15–60 hours keeps the rhythm of life recognizable
- **Year length** — too short and seasons lose meaning, too long and a human lifetime spans only a few years of seasonal experience. Something in the 200–800 day range keeps the cultural relationship to seasons legible
- **Moon count and behavior** — one to three moons with visible orbital periods creates rich sky mythology; more than that becomes chaotic and the sky stops being a clock
- **Axial tilt** — low tilt means weak seasons and equatorial civilizations dominate; high tilt means extreme seasons and polar civilizations either die or become extraordinarily hardy. Earth's 23° is a sweet spot but anything in the 10°–40° range produces interesting variation
- **Stellar color** — an orange K-dwarf star makes the sky different colors, affects photosynthesis, changes what "a clear day" looks like; this is one of the most visceral alienness levers available while keeping the world physically stable

The game can apply soft constraints during stellar formation that bias toward this habitable parameter space while still allowing genuine variation. The player isn't picking these — they're *nudging* a process that has its own physics, and the physics is tuned to produce interesting worlds more often than not. When it doesn't — when the world is a frozen rock or a Venus-equivalent — that's a different kind of awe. The universe didn't care.

## The God Who Is Also Small

The specific emotional experience you want is rare in games. The closest examples are:

**Dwarf Fortress** — where you build something intricate and the simulation destroys it in ways you couldn't have anticipated, and you feel both the grief of loss and the astonishment at what the simulation was capable of doing. The world is more alive than you managed it.

**Outer Wilds** — where every discovery makes the universe feel *larger* rather than smaller. Each answer opens three new questions. You never feel like you're running out of universe.

**Elite Dangerous in the black** — pure spatial awe, the feeling of being genuinely small against astronomical distances, with no gameplay to cushion it.

Truth sits in a category none of these quite occupy: you are *authoring* the thing that makes you feel small. That's theologically interesting — it's the creator's paradox, the experience of making something that exceeds you. The player guides the nebula toward a star and then watches the star burn for four billion years and warms a small world and complexity arises and the complexity starts telling stories about the very star the player made, stories the player didn't write, and those stories are *about* the nature of existence and they're *wrong in exactly the ways early humans were wrong* — animistic, astrological, mythological — and they're beautiful.

The player made the sun. The civilization made the sun a god. The player didn't make the god. The physics did.

That gap — between what the player made and what the simulation made from it — is where the awe lives. Every design decision should protect and widen that gap, never close it.

## A Single Practical Principle

From all of this, one design principle that should govern every system: **the player should always be able to see further than they can reach**. The observable horizon of the simulation should always extend beyond the actionable horizon. You can see the civilization forming on the far continent, but you can't act on it directly — you can only act on what's near, and watch the consequences propagate. You can see the stellar neighborhood, but you can't gate to it yet. You can see your avatar's descendants, but you can't control them.

Visibility without control is the engine of awe. It's the feeling of standing at the edge of what you made and realizing the view goes on without you.

## The Physics Stack for Phase 0

You need four interacting systems. These map cleanly to your existing namespace law:

**Gravity** (`domain/stellar.clj`) — every particle mass exerts attraction. At nebular scale this is an N-body approximation over statistical clouds rather than per-particle, collapsing to full resolution only in the focused cone. The same gravity code that collapses a gas cloud eventually moves rivers downhill and makes a player-character's body fall.

**Thermodynamics** (`domain/atmosphere.clj` seeded from `domain/stellar.clj`) — temperature as a property of particle kinetic energy. At nebular scale, gas compression heats. Radiative cooling distributes outward. The ignition of fusion is a threshold event: when a protostellar core exceeds ~10 million K and pressure exceeds ~10²⁵ Pa, hydrogen fusion begins. This is not scripted — it falls out of the simulation when conditions are met.

**Chemistry** (`domain/chemistry.clj`, new) — elemental abundance from nucleosynthesis. The starting nebula composition follows stellar population statistics. Heavier elements require prior stellar generation. Chemistry drives which minerals form on cooling planets, what early atmosphere composition looks like, whether liquid water is stable, what the preconditions for organic chemistry are. Same system, cooled and slowed, drives geology and eventually metabolism.

**Pressure** — emergent from thermodynamics and gravity interacting. Hydrostatic equilibrium is the exact threshold that defines a planet from a rubble pile. You get this for free when gravity and thermal pressure balance.

The beautiful thing is that **fusion ignition, planetary differentiation, and the emergence of hydrostatic equilibrium are all the same kind of event** — a threshold crossing in the same underlying physics. The game doesn't need to announce "a star has formed." The simulation crosses a threshold, light begins emitting, and the player sees it happen.

## Voxels as Universal Substrate

The voxel/particle duality is the right call architecturally. The principle is:

**Anything outside the player's focus cone is a statistical field. Anything inside it is a voxel mass.**

At Phase 0 scale, the "focus cone" is enormous — maybe a light-year across, centered on the nebular region of interest. Most of it is still statistical. But where the player's spark attends, particles resolve. This is the same system that will later mean: the river you're standing next to erodes its bank in voxel detail, but the river twenty kilometers away is a flow-rate number in `domain/hydrology.clj`.

The voxel representation doesn't change between phases. The *scale* of a voxel changes. A Phase 0 voxel might represent 10¹⁵ kg of gas. A Phase 5 voxel might represent a cubic meter of soil. The physics operations — pressure, thermal exchange, gravitational attraction, chemical reaction — are the same functions operating on different magnitudes.

This gives you the consistent control feel you're looking for. The player navigates space, focuses attention, and interacts with whatever is in focus. The meaning of "interact" changes. The interface doesn't.

## The Sprite — Attention as a Resource

You are a quantum oscillation. A spark. Specifically, you are a **coherent fluctuation in the quantum vacuum** — which is a real thing. Virtual particles constantly appear and annihilate in pairs. Most are instantaneous. Something about you persists. You don't know why yet. Neither does the narrator.

The resource isn't energy in the conventional game sense. It's **coherence** — the degree to which your quantum state remains distinguishable from background vacuum noise. This maps to several mechanics:

- **Focus cost**: attending to a region at high resolution costs coherence. Spreading your attention diffusely costs almost nothing but produces only statistical influence.
- **Coherence regeneration**: you gain coherence when you witness threshold events — stellar ignition, planetary formation, phase transitions. The universe rewarding observation is the loop.
- **Decoherence limit**: if you spread too thin, or stay in regions with no threshold events occurring, coherence drains. Time begins to slip. You don't die — you fade into the background and the camera detaches.
- **Jumping**: when a nebula is failing — no threshold events approaching, coherence near zero — the player can release focus and the spark drifts to another active nebula. This is not a menu. It's a physical act. The sprite simply lets go and the universe carries it somewhere interesting.

The narrator first manifests as a feeling. Then as a pattern in the particle noise. Then as a voice that seems to come from the simulation itself. The player may never address it directly. That's fine. It continues narrating regardless.

## The Narrator's Discovery Arc

The narrator should not announce itself. It should be discovered through three escalating recognitions:

**Recognition 1 — Pattern**: the player notices that certain particle arrangements seem to form shapes that look almost like letters, or that sounds from the simulation have a rhythm that feels almost intentional. This is the narrator trying to establish contact at the physics layer.

**Recognition 2 — Meaning**: during a threshold event — a star igniting, perhaps — there is a phrase that emerges clearly. Not in a chat box. In the visual/audio environment. Something like: *"there it is."* Two words. Ambiguous. Could be the simulation. Could be something else.

**Recognition 3 — Interface**: the chat box appears. But it has already been there. A thin border at the edge of the screen that the player may have dismissed as UI chrome. The first time they click it, the narrator says something like: *"I wondered when you'd ask."*

If the player never clicks it, the narrator continues to exist as environmental voice only. The game is entirely playable that way. The chat interface is discovered, not given.

## Phase 0 Failure as Cinematic Conclusion

When a nebula disperses without stellar ignition — too low mass, too much angular momentum loss, no threshold crossing — the sequence plays as follows:

1. Coherence stops regenerating. The player feels this as reduced resolution around them.
2. The statistical cloud becomes visually more diffuse. Particles that were resolving begin returning to blur.
3. The narrator's voice (ambient or explicit) shifts register — from present-tense to past-tense narration. *"This one was trying."*
4. Time accelerates without announcement. The dispersal of the cloud over millions of years plays in seconds.
5. The focus releases. The spark drifts.
6. A new active region comes into view somewhere in the larger field.

The player didn't fail. They witnessed a universe that didn't coalesce. Then they went somewhere else.

## The Consistent Control Layer

Phase 0 establishes four controls that never change meaning, only scale:


| Control | Phase 0 meaning | Phase 3 meaning | Phase 6 meaning |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Camera navigation** | Drifting through a nebular field | Surveying a biosphere from above | Walking through a city street |
| **Focus/attend** | Collapsing probability clouds into particle resolution | Zooming into an ecosystem in detail | Examining an object up close |
| **Interact** | Nudging particle density in focused region | Encouraging a migration or ecological pressure | Picking up a rock, opening a door |
| **Release/drift** | Letting attention spread back to statistical field | Pulling back to planetary view | Stepping back, disengaging |

The player learns these four operations in Phase 0 when stakes are cosmological and mistakes are beautiful rather than punishing. By the time the same operations mean "walk forward" and "pick up a rock," they are already fluent.

## Where Life Comes From

The ecology emergence pipeline from Phase 0 is a chain of threshold crossings that the chemistry system produces naturally:

**Stellar ignition → photon flux** → energy gradient available for chemistry → **planetary surface cooling below 100°C** → liquid water stability window → **organic molecule formation** (amino acid precursors form in interstellar space already — this is real) → **self-replicating chemistry threshold** → metabolism → **cellular structure** → evolution.

Each arrow is a threshold. The simulation doesn't script life. It runs chemistry until chemistry finds a configuration that self-perpetuates. Given the right conditions, this is close to inevitable. Given wrong conditions, you get a beautiful sterile world with interesting geology.

The player's soft boundary is felt when the chemistry is running and the thermal window is either too hot, too cold, or closing too fast. The narrator might say something. Or the colors might shift. Or the coherence drain rate increases slightly. The player *feels* that this world isn't finding its way, before they're told anything.

That feeling — of sensing without being informed — is where the awe lives.

# Gates of Truth — Phase 0 Stellar Nebula Slice

This document commits the implementation design for the first playable slice of **Gates of Truth**: the stellar nebula phase in which the player witnesses a star system form in front of them and learns, mostly without explicit tutorialization, what kind of being they are in this universe. The current repository already defines the four-quadrant architecture (`domain/`, `infra/`, `shape/`, `law/`), the requirement that domain logic stay pure, and the expectation that new systems be specified with law, tests, and then implementation.

## Purpose

Phase 0 must achieve five things at once:

1. Show a universe-forming process directly and convincingly.
2. Establish the underlying physics substrate that later phases continue rather than replace.
3. Introduce the player’s mode of agency as limited, subtle, and awe-oriented.
4. Teach the control language of the game without breaking immersion.
5. Produce a star system outcome that can continue toward habitability or gracefully fail into a witnessed conclusion.

The design target is not a menu-driven setup flow. It is an observable cosmological event sequence that the player can mostly watch, gently influence, and emotionally inhabit.

## Slice Definition

### Entry condition

The slice begins with a nebular mass distribution already present in simulation space. The player appears as a coherent spark-like observer embedded in that field.

### Exit condition

The slice ends when one of the following becomes true:

- A stable star forms and the surrounding system resolves into candidate planets and non-planets under the astronomical definition of a planet.
- The nebula fails to produce a viable life-bearing trajectory and the simulation transitions into a witnessed conclusion.
- The player’s attention/coherence can no longer sustain meaningful focus on the region, and the spark releases into another nebular site.


### Planet criterion

A body counts as a planet in this phase only when it satisfies the design’s intended astronomical meaning:

- It is in hydrostatic equilibrium.
- It has cleared its orbital neighborhood.

The player does not need this definition presented as UI text. The system uses it internally to determine which bodies survive phase transition.

## Experience Goals

### 1. A universe forms visibly

The player must be able to watch matter gather, heat, compress, ignite, flatten into a disk, collide, differentiate, and clear into orbital structure. The sequence should feel like a real process, not a scripted montage.

### 2. Awe through partial power

The player can influence outcomes, but never command them outright. Attention matters, timing matters, focus matters, but physics remains sovereign.

### 3. Soft boundaries

The game should communicate, through feel rather than explicit failure text, when a created system is no longer likely to lead to life. The player senses a narrowing of future possibility before the system is formally concluded.

### 4. One control language across scales

Camera drift, focus, interaction, and release are established here in a cosmological context and later persist at planetary, ecological, civilizational, and embodied scales.

## Design Thesis

Phase 0 is not a disposable prologue. It is the first expression of the universal simulation substrate.

The same world model introduced here must remain conceptually continuous with later systems:

- Particle and voxel matter becomes geology, hydrology, and material interaction later.
- Thermal gradients become climate and habitability constraints later.
- Chemistry becomes mineralogy, atmosphere, water, and eventually metabolism later.
- The player’s focus mechanic becomes ecological attention, cultural narrowing, and finally embodied perception later.

The player should come away feeling that they did not switch games. The universe merely cooled, slowed, and became more articulate.

## Simulation Model

## Matter Representation

Phase 0 uses a dual representation:

- **Statistical field** for unfocused or distant regions.
- **Voxel-particle resolved mass** for focused regions where local physics matters.

This maintains continuity with the later simulation architecture while making large-scale cosmology tractable.

### Representation rule

Anything outside the player’s focus volume is modeled statistically. Anything inside it can be promoted into discrete resolved matter suitable for local interaction and visualization.

### Consequence

The player’s attention is not just a camera feature. It is a computational and ontological act. Focus causes matter and events to become more detailed, more expensive, and more knowable.

## Core Physical Domains for this slice

### Gravity

Gravity drives collapse, orbital capture, accretion, and clearing. It must be present from the first frame of the slice.

Implementation intent:

- Use approximate N-body / field hybrid behavior for distant masses.
- Use more local direct interactions inside focused regions.
- Keep the API aligned with later use in orbital and geological systems.


### Temperature

Temperature emerges from compression, collision, radiative input, and cooling. This is the primary bridge between collapse and ignition, and later between sunlight and habitability.

Implementation intent:

- Track temperature as a first-class scalar on resolved matter regions.
- Support heating through compression and collisions.
- Support cooling through radiation and expansion.


### Pressure

Pressure determines whether matter remains diffuse, collapses further, or reaches the thresholds needed for ignition and hydrostatic equilibrium.

Implementation intent:

- Derive pressure from density and temperature in the minimal slice.
- Use hydrostatic equilibrium tests as part of planet classification.


### Chemistry

Chemistry determines what matter can later become: rocky bodies, volatile-rich bodies, atmospheres, oceans, and prebiotic environments.

Implementation intent:

- Start with a minimal elemental inventory sufficient for cosmological and planetary differentiation: hydrogen, helium, oxygen, carbon, silicon, iron, and a coarse “heavy elements” bucket if needed.
- Track composition statistically for distant matter and explicitly for focused matter.


### Fusion and light

Fusion is the threshold event that turns a collapsing protostar into a star and changes the meaning of every later physical process.

Implementation intent:

- Treat ignition as a consequence of temperature, pressure, and composition crossing threshold conditions.
- Once fusion begins, emit light and radiative energy as simulation-visible outputs.
- Light must not be merely decorative; it must become a real driver of later thermal differentiation.


## Minimal World State for the Slice

The slice needs a pure simulation state that can later evolve rather than be discarded. Conceptually it should include:

- Nebular regions.
- Resolved matter clusters.
- Proto-stars and stars.
- Accretion disk structures.
- Orbiting bodies.
- Player spark state.
- Focus volume.
- Coherence/attention resource state.
- Slice phase markers that are internal to the simulation, not tutorial labels shown to the player.

This should become the basis for the law schema and test fixtures, consistent with the repository’s design rule that new systems begin with schema and tests before implementation.

## Player Form and Resource

## Player ontology

In this slice, the player is a coherent luminous fluctuation: a spark, sprite, or quantum oscillation in the vacuum. They are not yet a person and not quite a god.

This means their influence should feel like:

- Bias rather than command.
- Attention rather than tool use.
- Presence rather than ownership.


## Resource concept

The best current resource frame is **coherence**, expressed experientially as attention sustained against vacuum noise.

Why this works:

- It fits the cosmological fiction.
- It explains why focused observation has cost.
- It supports gentle failure states through decoherence rather than hard death.
- It scales conceptually into later forms of agency.


### Coherence behaviors

- Focusing on a region consumes coherence.
- Witnessing threshold events can restore coherence.
- Remaining in low-novelty or dying systems drains coherence over time.
- If coherence is exhausted and no regeneration route exists, the player loses the ability to sustain meaningful influence and becomes primarily observer.


## Control Model

The control language introduced here must survive into later phases with evolving meaning.


| Control | Phase 0 expression | Later continuity |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Drift | Move the spark through nebular space | Move over a planet, through a region, or as an embodied character |
| Focus | Collapse a region into detail and attention | Inspect terrain, ecology, people, or objects |
| Influence | Bias local physical conditions within narrow constraints | Bias ecology, culture, or act as a person |
| Release | Pull back from a region and let it return to statistical distance | Exit close observation, de-escalate control, or widen scope |

The player should feel that these controls are simply becoming more intimate and more local as the universe cools.

## Visibility and Focus

Focus is the heart of both presentation and computation.

### Focus volume

The player always has a current region of strongest observational presence. This focus volume:

- Promotes matter into higher-resolution simulation.
- Enables direct local influence.
- Determines where the richest visuals and audio occur.
- Shapes what the player learns implicitly.


### Design consequence

The player never sees everything equally. This preserves awe, keeps distant regions statistical, and gives attention mechanical meaning.

## Emergent Event Sequence

The slice should reliably create a readable sequence of emergent events, even if their exact timing and outcomes vary:

1. Diffuse nebular drift.
2. Local gravitational concentration.
3. Compression heating.
4. Protostellar collapse.
5. Disk formation.
6. Ignition threshold crossing.
7. Accretion and collision among orbiting bodies.
8. Differentiation into planets, dwarf-like remnants, belts, and debris.
9. Orbital clearing and stable system settlement.
10. Habitability potential assessment for onward simulation.

These are simulation realities, not chapter cards.

## Soft Transition to Ecology

Phase 0 must already contain the seeds of ecology without skipping prematurely to life.

The slice therefore needs to produce, for each surviving planet-scale body, at least a first-pass answer to the following questions:

- Is the body rocky, icy, gaseous, or mixed?
- Does it fall into a plausible thermal band for later habitability?
- Does it retain an atmosphere, and of what rough class?
- Is liquid solvent stability plausible under future cooling conditions?
- Are the bulk chemistry and orbital circumstances compatible with later prebiotic complexity?

The point is not to generate life here. The point is to hand later slices a physically grounded starting point from which life can plausibly emerge.

## Habitability and Soft Boundaries

The player must sense when a system is narrowing toward sterile beauty rather than future life.

This should be communicated through:

- The evolving color and texture of worlds.
- The kinds of threshold events that do or do not occur.
- The recovery rate of coherence.
- The narrator’s tone, if perceived.
- The degree to which the simulation keeps presenting branching potential.

Hard fail text should be avoided. Instead, possibility thins out.

### Examples of soft-boundary signals

- A protostar ignites but the habitable zone contains no durable rocky worlds.
- Accretion yields only unstable orbital dynamics and repeated sterilizing collisions.
- Chemistry favors worlds too volatile, too dry, too massive, or too cold for later complexity.
- The player’s interventions stop creating new meaningful divergences.

These do not mean the slice is worthless. They mean the player is approaching a witnessed conclusion rather than a life-bearing continuation.

## Failure, Drift, and Multi-Nebula Flow

A failed nebula must still be beautiful to watch.

When no viable path remains:

1. Coherence regeneration diminishes.
2. Interaction opportunities become sparse.
3. The player spends more time watching than shaping.
4. Time subtly accelerates.
5. The system resolves into its conclusion.
6. The spark drifts or is drawn to another active nebular site.

This makes repeated universe watching a natural part of the experience rather than a retry loop framed as punishment.

## Narrator / AI Presence

The player should be able to complete the slice without ever explicitly using a chat interface.

### Discovery approach

The AI storyteller should enter the experience in layers:

- First as a pattern in the audiovisual field.
- Then as occasional meaningful phrasing embedded into events.
- Only later as something recognizable as an addressable presence.


### Design rule

The AI exists whether or not the player talks back.

This preserves the feeling that the universe is accompanied, interpreted, and gently witnessed from within, while leaving room for players who never want explicit conversational interaction.

## Rendering and Feel

The player’s stated goal for this slice is to **see a universe form in front of their eyes**. That implies presentation constraints, even for a terminal-forward project.

The slice should prioritize:

- Readable large-scale matter motion.
- Visible heating and cooling changes.
- A clear visual event when fusion begins.
- Strong sense of scale without requiring labels.
- A luminous player-spark that remains legible but never dominates the scene.

Rendering remains an `infra/` responsibility in this repository, and any visualization should consume pure world-state rather than embed simulation logic directly.

## Namespace Plan

The current repository guidance implies the following initial slice boundaries:

### `law/`

- `law/stellar.clj` for world-state schemas and invariants.
- `law/chemistry.clj` if chemistry records are distinct enough to warrant dedicated contracts.


### `domain/`

- `domain/stellar.clj` for nebula collapse, gravity approximations, ignition thresholds, accretion flow.
- `domain/chemistry.clj` for composition blending, thermal class implications, and early material categorization.
- Potential shared interfaces via `defprotocol` if tick/update behavior needs explicit simulation roles.


### `infra/`

- `infra/render/stellar.clj` or equivalent for slice visualization, player spark rendering, and event presentation.
- Input mapping for drift, focus, influence, and release.
- Narrator presentation layer and discoverable chat shell.


### `shape/`

- Spatial helpers for focus volume, camera projection, and local field sampling if needed.


## First Implementation Slice

The first vertical slice should be as small as possible while still emotionally honest.

### Must-have behaviors

- A diffuse nebula visible on screen.
- A controllable spark-like player presence.
- Focus that changes simulation detail and visual emphasis.
- Gravitational collapse in at least one region.
- Compression heating.
- A threshold event for ignition.
- A visible accretion structure around the star.
- Formation of at least a few orbiting body classes.
- Coherence as a spend/recover resource.
- A graceful drift-away path if the system is non-viable.


### May-wait behaviors

- Full chemical realism.
- Precise multi-star dynamics.
- Exhaustive orbital clearing logic.
- Rich narrator interactivity.
- Final habitability scoring sophistication.

The first goal is not astrophysical completion. The first goal is a watchable, influenceable genesis event that proves the design language.

## Test Strategy

The repository’s guidance is explicit: new systems should be introduced as schema, then failing tests, then implementation.
